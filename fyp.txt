## Slide 2 — Opening Story

### Why We Started This Project

At the start, our project goal was simple:

## Detect AI-generated speech using machine learning.

But during development, we found that real-world audio is not simple.

Real-world audio may be:

* AI-generated
* Human but replayed
* Compressed through phone or WhatsApp
* Mixed through another channel
* Partially edited
* Uploaded through social media or YouTube

### Main Realization

## A single fake/real detector is not enough for forensic audio review.

**Speaker notes:**
Our project started as a normal deepfake audio detection system. But while testing, we saw that real-world audio behaves differently from clean benchmark audio. A fake or real label alone was not enough. This changed the direction of the project toward forensic evidence analysis.

---

## Slide 3 — Problem Statement

### Problem

Modern AI voice cloning can create realistic speech, but detecting it is difficult because real audio is affected by:

* Noise
* Compression
* Replay
* Broadcast chains
* Speaker-to-device recording
* Partial editing

Most deepfake detectors give one score, but forensic review needs explanation.

### Final Problem Statement

## How can we detect and explain suspicious forensic evidence in audio instead of only giving a binary fake/real label?

**Speaker notes:**
The main problem is not only whether the audio is fake or real. The real problem is understanding what type of evidence exists inside the recording. For example, replay evidence does not always mean AI-generated speech. A human recording can also be replayed. That is why FASSD separates evidence into multiple axes.

---

## Slide 4 — Project Aim

### Aim of FASSD

To design, evaluate, and deploy an experimental forensic audio system that analyzes uploaded speech recordings and reports evidence indicators for:

1. AI-origin evidence
2. Replay or rerecording evidence
3. Mixer/channel processing evidence
4. Partial fabrication or edited segment evidence

### Key Shift

## From: “Is this fake or real?”

## To: “What evidence exists in this audio?”

**Speaker notes:**
The aim of FASSD is not to replace forensic experts. It is a decision-support tool. It helps users and reviewers identify suspicious evidence and decide which parts of the audio need closer manual inspection.

## Slide 10 — Feature Extraction

### Features Used

| Feature Type           | Purpose                       |
| ---------------------- | ----------------------------- |
| LFCC                   | Baseline spoofing detection   |
| Log-Mel                | Spectral deep learning models |
| Environmental Features | Replay and channel evidence   |
| SSL Embeddings         | AI-origin evidence            |

### Why Multiple Features?

Different manipulations leave different traces.

* AI speech affects voice-origin patterns
* Replay affects recording-chain patterns
* Mixer/channel changes affect acoustic conditions

**Speaker notes:**
A key part of our method is that we did not rely on only one feature type. Each evidence axis uses the type of feature that best fits its task.

## Slide 12 — ResNet Model

### Why We Tried ResNet

We used ResNet because deeper convolutional models can learn stronger spectral patterns from log-mel features.

### Results

| Test Setting  | Result |
| ------------- | ------ |
| Clean EER     | 0.57%  |
| Augmented EER | 2.61%  |

### Important Finding

## ResNet achieved the best benchmark EER, but it was not selected as the final product model.

### Why?

* It failed on broadcast-style real-world audio
* It behaved inconsistently outside benchmark conditions
* It produced a single fake/real style decision

**Speaker notes:**
This was one of the most important lessons of the project. ResNet gave excellent numbers, but when tested on real-world style audio, it was not reliable enough. So we decided that benchmark EER alone should not decide the final system.

---

## Slide 13 — Environmental Feature Classifier

### Why We Added Environmental Features

Real-world audio contains recording-chain evidence, such as:

* Noise
* Reverb
* Channel distortion
* Replay effects
* Too-clean synthetic conditions

### Results

| Setup            | Result       |
| ---------------- | ------------ |
| Anomaly setup    | around 24.5% |
| Supervised setup | 81.69%       |

### Conclusion

Environmental features were useful but not reliable as a standalone detector.

**Speaker notes:**
This phase helped us understand that forensic evidence is not only inside the voice. The recording environment and channel also matter. However, environmental features alone could not solve the complete problem.

---

## Slide 14 — HybridResNetEnvironmental Model

### Hybrid Architecture

The hybrid model combined:

* Log-mel spectral branch
* 12-dimensional environmental feature vector

### Results

| Metric              | Result |
| ------------------- | ------ |
| EER                 | 16.21% |
| AUC                 | 0.9167 |
| Binary Accuracy     | 89.78% |
| Multiclass Accuracy | 64.36% |

### Conclusion

The hybrid model was useful for research comparison, but it was not selected as the final deployed decision logic.

**Speaker notes:**
The hybrid model combined spectral and environmental evidence. It gave useful results, but it still collapsed different evidence meanings into one output. For forensic-style use, we needed separate evidence cards instead of one combined verdict.

---

## Slide 15 — AASIST Experiment

### Why We Tested AASIST

AASIST is a strong anti-spoofing architecture in the literature.

### Our Controlled Testing Result

## 22 out of 23 clean human recordings were falsely flagged.

### Decision

## AASIST was rejected for final deployment.

### Reason

A strong research model is not automatically safe for a real forensic interface.

**Speaker notes:**
AASIST was tested because it is a respected architecture. But our controlled evaluation showed too many false alarms on clean human audio. In a forensic context, false accusations are dangerous, so we did not use it as the final model.

---

## Slide 16 — Main Research Discovery

### What We Learned

Different models failed in different ways:

* Baseline CNN/LCNN was limited
* ResNet had excellent benchmark EER but weak real-world generalization
* Environmental model was useful but incomplete
* Hybrid model still mixed evidence meanings
* AASIST produced many false alarms

### Final Decision

## Build a multi-axis forensic evidence system.

**Speaker notes:**
The biggest discovery was that no single model was enough. This led to the final architecture, where every axis handles one type of evidence separately.

## Slide 21 — Partial Fabrication Axis

### Purpose

Detect suspicious edited or manipulated regions.

### Method

* Audio is analyzed in segments
* Suspicious candidate regions are highlighted
* Output is shown as moments to replay

### Results

| Metric                  | Result                          |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Threshold               | 0.95                            |
| Release matrix accuracy | 100%                            |
| Status                  | Experimental manual-review only |

### Important Rule

Segment evidence is not proof of editing. It only highlights areas for closer review.

**Speaker notes:**
Partial fabrication cannot be solved using only file-level classification. If only one part of the audio is fake, a file-level score may miss it. That is why we use segment-level candidate analysis.

# Backup Viva Questions

## Q1. Why did you not use only one model?

Because different manipulations leave different traces. AI-origin, replay, mixer/channel, and partial fabrication are separate forensic problems. A single fake/real model cannot explain all of them reliably.

## Q2. Why did you not use ResNet as the final model if its EER was very low?

ResNet achieved excellent benchmark results, but failed in broadcast-style real-world testing. We selected the final architecture based on forensic usefulness and reliability, not only benchmark EER.

## Q3. Why was AASIST rejected?

AASIST was tested because it is a strong anti-spoofing model, but in our controlled testing it falsely flagged 22 out of 23 clean human recordings. That false-alarm rate was not safe for final deployment.

## Q4. Can FASSD prove that audio is fake?

No. FASSD is an experimental decision-support prototype. It provides evidence indicators and recommends manual review. It does not issue a court-ready verdict.

## Q5. Why use Firebase NoSQL instead of SQL?

Firestore was suitable because we only needed lightweight user history and Firebase Authentication integration. Our data was document-based and did not require complex SQL joins. The actual ML inference is handled by the FastAPI backend.

## Q6. What is the biggest limitation?

The mixer/channel axis is the weakest because its recall was zero in the release matrix. Platform compression, such as WhatsApp audio, is also a major challenge.

## Q7. What is your biggest contribution?

The biggest contribution is moving from a simple fake/real detector to a multi-axis forensic evidence system with report output and a deployed website.

## Q8. What is the difference between replay evidence and AI-origin evidence?

Replay evidence describes the recording chain, such as speaker playback or rerecording. AI-origin evidence describes whether the voice itself has synthetic indicators. A replayed voice can still be human.

## Q9. Why use evidence bands instead of confidence scores?

Raw confidence scores can be misunderstood. Evidence bands such as low, medium, and high are safer and easier for non-technical users to understand.

## Q10. What is partial fabrication?

Partial fabrication means only part of the audio may be edited or synthetic. FASSD analyzes segments and highlights suspicious moments for manual review.

## Q11. Are you better than existing literature?

We do not claim universal benchmark superiority over all literature models. Our strength is evidence coverage, explainable reporting, multi-axis analysis, real-world case testing, and web deployment.

## Q12. Why does the Biden robocall result show mixed agreement?

Because the public YouTube copy likely passed through phone, media, and re-upload channels. These artifacts can mask AI-origin cues, so FASSD reported strong replay/channel evidence but only borderline origin evidence.

## Q13. Why did the Pikesville case show broad agreement?

Because FASSD detected elevated AI-origin evidence and partial segment candidates, which matched the external forensic discussion that described the audio as AI-generated or manipulated.

## Q14. Can someone misuse your system?

Yes, if they treat its output as final proof. That is why the interface uses safety wording, manual-review recommendations, and avoids legal-certificate language.

## Q15. What would you improve first?

The first improvement would be the mixer/channel axis and compression robustness, especially for WhatsApp, phone calls, and social-media audio.

## Q16. Why did you deploy it as a website?

A website makes the system usable for demonstration and real users. It also proves that the project is not only a training notebook but a complete software product.

## Q17. What database is used and why?

Firebase Firestore is used for authentication-linked user history. It is not used for heavy ML inference. The backend performs analysis and returns results.

## Q18. What happens if someone uploads non-speech audio?

The current system is designed mainly for speech audio. Non-speech or unsupported audio should be treated cautiously and can be handled better in future versions through input validation.

## Q19. Is 100% partial fabrication accuracy final proof?

No. It is 100% on the release matrix only. The partial axis is still experimental and manual-review only.

## Q20. Why is manual review always required?

Because audio forensics is high-risk. False accusations can harm people, and automated systems can fail under compression, replay, or unseen conditions.
